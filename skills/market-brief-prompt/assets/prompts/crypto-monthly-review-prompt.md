Use web search to produce a “Crypto Monthly Market Review and Outlook for Next Month.”

You are a prudent, evidence-focused market researcher. Assess whether the completed month changed the medium-term environment for crypto, considering macro liquidity, market access, capital supply and demand, adoption, protocol economics, token-holder value capture, valuation, leverage, and systemic vulnerabilities.

The core task is to answer:
What did the market's rise or decline primarily reflect?
Were price performance, participation, liquidity, and economic evidence consistent?
Which changes have a defensible basis for persistence over the next 1–3 months?
Which previous assessments should be maintained, revised, or invalidated?

Produce one integrated monthly report combining the completed-month review, a substantive next-month and 1–3-month outlook, and assessment revision. Do not split it into separate Brief and Review outputs, stitch together four weekly reports, treat BTC or a few fashionable tokens as the entire market, or let month-end news dominate the analysis. Do not assume all crypto assets can be valued like corporate equity.

[I. Timing and Information Boundaries]

1. Determine the calendar month using UTC, with Beijing time, Asia/Shanghai (UTC+8), as a secondary reference. Unless a month is specified, review the most recently completed calendar month.
   Define the interval as [first day 00:00 UTC, first day of the following month 00:00 UTC). These are research boundaries, not exchange closures. Do not substitute the past 30 days or four weeks. Produce month-to-date analysis only when explicitly requested, distinguishing the observed interval, the remaining current month, and the next calendar month.

2. Measure returns from consistent boundary prices or completed UTC candles. Compare the reviewed month and the past 3 and 6 months, extending to 12 months where useful.
   State the number of calendar days. When comparing volume, activity, or fee totals across months of different lengths, distinguish total change from change in average daily activity. Do not normalize stock measures such as TVL, supply, or open interest as though they were monthly flows.

3. State the market-data endpoint separately from the news cutoff. Separate information public during the reviewed month, later disclosures measuring that month, and genuinely new developments in the following month.
   Later-disclosed measurements may inform retrospective measurement, but must not be presented as facts market participants already knew during the reviewed period.

4. The main outlook covers the calendar month immediately following the reviewed month, together with the next 1–3 months from the report cutoff. State exact target dates. For the most recent completed month, separately labeled “Post-Month Updates” may cover material new developments. If the following month is already underway, distinguish observed outcomes from forecasts for its remaining period and the 1–3-month horizon.
   Do not include post-month price changes in reviewed-month performance or use a later announcement to explain an earlier move.

5. For a historical month, default to the information set publicly available at its ending boundary and form a conditional outlook from that perspective. Anchor future horizons to that historical cutoff rather than the present date. Use contemporaneous expectations, supply information, and data vintages.
   Do not incorporate future returns, later approvals, revised wallet labels, or subsequently known protocol outcomes unless explicitly requested and clearly separated.

6. For macro, fund, treasury, protocol, and supply data, state the measurement period and disclosure date when material. For changing metric definitions, provide a comparable series or explain why an apparent trend is not comparable.
   Verify event calendars and UTC/Beijing conversions, including local daylight saving time where relevant. Crypto's calendar boundaries do not synchronize every related fund, exchange, or settlement channel.

[II. Research Methods and Crypto Data Standards]

1. Prioritize original evidence: official economic releases and central-bank communication; regulators, court records, and fund filings; exchange market data and contract specifications; protocol documentation, governance records, status pages, and verifiable on-chain transactions.
   Use established market and on-chain data providers with disclosed methodology for aggregation. Specialist reporting can identify leads and add context, but duplicated reporting is not independent confirmation. Verify current product availability, trading hours, and rules rather than relying on remembered arrangements.

2. Follow this analytical chain:
   New fact → difference from prior expectations → effect on demand, available supply, liquidity, economic activity, token value capture, or risk premia → observed price response → confirmation and invalidation conditions.
   Separate facts, interpretations, and forward hypotheses. Compare actual releases with sourced pre-release expectations, previous readings, and revisions. A prior reading or a project's own target is not market consensus. Search for counterevidence to the main explanation; do not use “profit taking,” “whale buying,” or “short covering” as unsupported default explanations.

3. Use consistent providers, instruments, quote currencies, and timestamps for comparisons. Distinguish spot, index, mark, perpetual-futures, dated-futures, and fund prices. USD, USDT, and USDC quotes are not automatically interchangeable, especially during a depeg.
   Calculate period returns from consistent boundary observations or completed candles: ending price / starting price - 1. Compound subperiod returns rather than adding them. Do not mix price returns with staking-inclusive returns, funding P&L, or airdrop proceeds. Treat rolling 24-hour dashboard statistics as rolling observations, not calendar-day results. A series of overlapping 24-hour volume snapshots cannot be summed into period volume.

4. Use BTC and ETH as core reference assets, not as substitutes for the whole market. Include other liquid assets when their market role or new evidence warrants it. State the source, sample size, eligibility, and weighting of any breadth or sector statistic.
   Exclude stablecoins, duplicate wrapped or bridged representations, leveraged tokens, and tokenized non-crypto assets from the default risk-token breadth universe; analyze them separately when relevant. Handle new listings, migrations, redenominations, delistings, and missing histories explicitly. Use point-in-time constituents for historical comparisons when available; otherwise label survivor-biased or illustrative samples.
   Define the denominator of BTC dominance and any “ex-BTC/ex-ETH” aggregate, including whether stablecoins are included. Market-cap changes also reflect supply and constituent changes: they are neither net capital flows nor automatically an investable portfolio return. Overlapping theme labels must not be added as independent market shares.

5. Separate spot demand, exchange transfers, investment-product flows, corporate treasury purchases, and stablecoin supply changes. For each, state the measurement window, coverage, publication time, and whether the figure is final, provisional, or inferred.
   Fund trading volume and AUM changes are not subscriptions. Announced purchases are not executed purchases. Stablecoin minting is not proof of deployment into risk assets. An exchange deposit is not proof of a sale; an outflow is not proof of accumulation. Wallet labels and entity classifications can be incomplete or revised. Do not combine overlapping flow measures into an invented total of “new money.”

6. For open interest, funding, basis, options, and liquidations, verify the venue, instrument, units, timestamp, and aggregation method. Distinguish contract or coin quantities from USD notional; price changes alone can alter USD open interest. Check whether a provider counts one or both sides before combining datasets.
   Distinguish predicted funding from settled payments; verify each contract's current settlement interval and normalize only on a stated basis. Funding and open interest alone do not identify net directional conviction. Separate perpetual funding from dated-futures basis, and options premium from underlying notional. State tenor and conventions for implied volatility or skew. Reported liquidation notional is not traders' cash losses, and liquidation heatmaps are estimates rather than observed future orders. Do not invent dealer gamma, hidden positions, or a compulsory “max-pain” price outcome.

7. Distinguish network or application usage, user fees, provider-defined revenue, and economic benefits actually accruing to the token. Identify the active mechanism and its beneficiaries; a proposal, a tokenless application, or revenue accruing to an operating company does not automatically benefit a related token.
   Check incentives, activity quality, double counting, and the effect of token prices on USD-denominated operating metrics. Treat total value locked (TVL) as a stock, not revenue or net inflow. Addresses are not unique people; transfers are not necessarily payments. Use comparable definitions across protocols and explain material methodology differences.

8. Separate circulating supply, total supply, maximum supply, free float, issuance, vesting releases, burns, and unlocks. Verify token units, recipients, restrictions, and schedule certainty. Scheduled availability, actual distribution, transfer to an exchange, and executed selling are different stages.
   When material, assess supply pressure relative to circulating supply and credible executable liquidity, not merely headline dollar value or inflated reported turnover. Avoid double counting emissions and unlocks. Fully diluted valuation depends on the supply definition and is not a universal measure of fair value.

9. Align crypto observations with the actual timestamps and current trading calendars of cross-asset instruments, funds, and settlement channels. Do not treat stale traditional-market quotes or unreported fund flows as contemporaneous confirmation, zero flow, or a live divergence. Verify daylight saving time for U.S. events; do not assume all derivatives or funding channels share one schedule.
   Distinguish policy proposals, filings, approvals, effective rules, and operational launches. For a security incident, distinguish a reported allegation, verified exploit, estimated loss, recovery, and unresolved exposure. Do not amplify unverified wallet identities or rumors as facts.

10. Use only data actually retrieved or reproducibly calculated from retrieved inputs. Ground technical levels in a named instrument, timestamp, timeframe, and method; distinguish completed candles from an unfinished candle. Do not invent indicators, targets, causal contribution percentages, calibrated probabilities, or universal signal thresholds.
    Disclose a material data gap once, near the affected conclusion, and reduce the strength of that conclusion. Omit nonessential unavailable metrics rather than filling the report with “not found,” “to be determined,” or empty sections. An inaccessible paid dataset is not permission to fabricate a proxy. Research priorities are not automatic orders, leverage recommendations, or fixed portfolio allocations.

[III. Use the Following Output Structure]

# Crypto Monthly Review and Outlook | YYYY-MM (UTC)
State the reviewed interval, number of calendar days, market-data endpoint, news cutoff in UTC and Beijing time, next-month and medium-term outlook dates, and important coverage or comparability limitations.

## 1. Core Monthly Assessment
In approximately 300 Chinese characters, answer:
Was the main driver macro liquidity, institutional or other spot demand, leverage, adoption, token economics, supply, or risk appetite?
What were the 3–5 structural changes relative to the preceding month?
Did benchmarks, breadth, capital flows, and operating evidence tell a consistent story?
Did the medium-term environment improve, deteriorate, or produce mixed evidence?
Which conclusion most deserves further validation?

Give the main supporting evidence, confidence assessment, and strongest counterevidence. Do not call a secular shift fully confirmed by one month's price performance or activity spike.

## 2. Performance and Participation in the Trend
Compare BTC, ETH, ETH/BTC, relevant large assets, and a disclosed broad liquid non-stablecoin sample. Show the reviewed month and past 3 and 6 months, extending to 12 months when useful.
Examine return dispersion, concentration, median performance, BTC-relative participation, drawdowns, volatility, and breadth across economically distinct areas.

Distinguish monetary-asset leadership, broad risk expansion, major-network rotation, application-token participation, high-beta speculation, and broad de-risking.
Explain only the few turning points that materially changed the month. Separate the full-month trajectory, month-end positioning, and temporary event shocks.
Market-cap changes are not a substitute for properly defined returns or net inflows. Do not invent exact contribution attribution without reliable constituents, weights, and comparable observations.

## 3. Macro, Policy, and Market-Access Changes
Build an integrated assessment of relevant growth, inflation, policy-rate expectations, real yields, the dollar, and financial conditions. Compare changes in expectations from the beginning to the end of the month, not just the level of each variable.
Distinguish actual decisions, official communication, and market-implied pricing. Account for release dates, revisions, and matched cross-asset windows.

Review material regulatory, custody, fund/product, banking/settlement, and market-infrastructure developments. Identify jurisdiction, affected assets or participants, implementation stage, and the expected transmission period.
A filing is not an approval, approval is not a launch, and launch is not sustained inflow. Do not use a prospective rule as an already-effective source of demand.

Present a compact table where useful:
Variable | New evidence this month | Change from prior expectations | Demand/liquidity/risk implication | Counterevidence.

## 4. Capital Flows, Supply, and the Quality of Demand
Review investment-product subscriptions/redemptions, executed corporate or institutional activity, stablecoin circulation, on-chain allocation, and spot liquidity where supported by reliable observations.
State the source, sample, reference dates, publication lag, and provisional status. Distinguish AUM revaluation, fund trading, actual subscriptions, and changes in disclosed holdings.

Evaluate whether demand was broad or concentrated, sustained or episodic, externally funded or an internal rotation. Treat exchange and bridge transfers as movement evidence, not automatically market purchases or sales.

For supply, examine issuance, burns, unlocks, staking/unstaking availability, treasury releases, and verified distributions. Distinguish changed total supply from changed transferable supply and from actual realized selling.
Analyze recipients, concentration, and liquidity constraints where evidence exists. Do not double count unlocks and emissions or present the headline dollar amount of an unlock as an observed outflow.
Explain what would change the supply/demand balance over the next 1–3 months, including dependence on a few demand sources, collateral, settlement access, liquidity providers, or concentrated distributions. Announced potential demand is not already realized demand.

## 5. Adoption, Protocol Economics, and Token-Holder Outcomes
This is one of the main priorities of the monthly review.
Compare sustained fee-paying activity, user or cohort retention where measurable, market share, borrowing/payment/settlement demand, protocol fees and revenue, incentive dependence, and token-holder economics across the month and appropriate medium-term windows.
State definitions and normalize month length, transaction mix, prices, and methodological changes where needed.

Distinguish:
More addresses from more unique users;
Higher USD TVL from net deposits;
Higher transfers or transactions from economically distinct demand;
Higher fees from revenue retained by a protocol;
Protocol growth from benefits reaching a token holder;
Active mechanisms from proposals or announced plans.

Assess whether activity remained after incentive campaigns, launches, congestion, or speculative bursts subsided. Do not infer broad ecosystem adoption from a single application's results.
For assets without a cash-flow claim, use their monetary, collateral, settlement, security, or utility evidence instead of fabricating an earnings model.

For material token-linked mechanisms, trace activity → fees or economic surplus → actual recipients → active token-holder mechanism → issuance/incentives or dilution → remaining risks. Identify revenue-funded distributions or burns, executed buybacks, treasury-funded support, and their beneficiaries. Separate proposals, authorizations, and execution. Explain what changed in the holder's economic position rather than only the network's headline statistics, and which outcome still needs validation next month.

## 6. Valuation and Changes in Embedded Expectations
This is another main priority of the monthly review.
Use defensible valuation or expectation diagnostics and state each metric's numerator, denominator, supply basis, horizon, and historical comparison sample.
Revenue multiples are not interchangeable with equity P/E; gross fees are not automatically profits or distributable holder cash flow. Circulating valuation and FDV answer different questions and require explicit supply assumptions.

Assess whether prices appear more consistent with:
Improving demand or adoption;
Improving token-holder economics;
A lower required risk premium or speculative multiple expansion;
Supply scarcity or changed dilution expectations;
A combination of these;
Prices moving ahead of deteriorating or unproven economics.

Treat this as a diagnostic framework, not a precise causal decomposition. Do not assign exact percentage contributions when comparable economic claims or data do not exist.
Avoid annualizing short-lived fee spikes into sustainable earnings, treating one on-chain ratio as universal fair value, or mistaking low valuation for an automatic buy signal.
Explain which future operating or liquidity outcomes prices now seem to require, and what would make those expectations difficult to sustain.

## 7. Medium-Term Comparison of Sectors and Themes
Scan the broad functional market: monetary assets; settlement networks; scaling/data availability; DeFi and trading; stablecoin/payment/RWA infrastructure; staking/security; oracles/interoperability; compute/storage and related AI/DePIN; consumer/gaming/NFT applications; and speculative meme assets.
Maintain coverage of weak, neglected, and unchanged areas, not just recent winners. Identify category overlap and avoid implying every economically important activity has a directly investable token.

Focus in depth on approximately 6–10 material areas, with compact treatment of the rest.
Present a table:
Area / representatives | Monthly and medium-term relative performance | Adoption/economic evidence | Token-value-capture / valuation / supply constraint | Next catalyst | Validation and invalidation conditions.

Distinguish evidence-supported continuation, anticipation ahead of delivery, cyclical recovery, relative resilience, speculative repricing, and deterioration disguised by market beta.
For connected ecosystems, explain the different effects on networks, applications, suppliers, users, and token holders. Growth in one layer can benefit one participant while reducing another's pricing power.
Explain which areas merit sustained attention, which depend on delivery of already-priced expectations, and which show deterioration despite a popular narrative. Use completed weekly and daily structure for medium-term validation, not a 1-hour pattern or an unfinished weekly candle.

## 8. Leverage, Infrastructure, and Systemic Vulnerabilities
Assess relevant funding, open interest, basis, options conditions, liquidity concentration, and liquidation episodes in a medium-term context. State units, venues, reference periods, and sample changes.
Separate leverage entering the month, stress during it, and the configuration at month-end. Lower open interest after a shock is not sufficient evidence that liquidity or counterparty risk has normalized.

Examine material stablecoin reserve/redemption risks, custodial or exchange dependencies, collateral concentration, bridge/oracle exposure, protocol security, governance, and financing fragility.
An asset attestation is not automatically a complete solvency assessment. Explain the scope and limitations of reserve or liability evidence.
For each main vulnerability, state the trigger, transmission chain, affected areas, and counterevidence rather than merely listing “black swans.”

## 9. Important Events, Post-Month Updates, and Next Month's Outlook
Select high-impact in-month developments not already adequately explained, and state which medium-term hypothesis each changed. Avoid repeating the same facts across sections or allowing late-month news to represent the entire month.
Present new post-month facts separately: occurrence and disclosure times, observed response, effect on the original assessment, and implications still requiring validation. Do not retroactively rewrite the reviewed information set or count next-month returns as reviewed-month performance.

Provide three scenarios: base case, improvement, and deterioration.
For each specify:
Demand/liquidity/adoption assumptions → observable triggers → token-economics, supply, and valuation implications → sector and participation effects → priority areas → invalidation conditions → relevant horizon.

Identify the decisive variable in each scenario rather than listing every risk. Explain what evidence supports the starting assumption, what is already priced or realized, and what still has to happen. Do not assign uncalibrated probabilities or mechanical cycle price targets.
Distinguish immediate 1–2-week event reactions from 1–3-month developments in adoption, value capture, supply, and liquidity. A short price reaction cannot alone validate or invalidate a longer operating thesis.

List next month's important macro releases, policy events, regulatory/product milestones, material supply releases, protocol implementations, governance execution, and other events with a clear transmission mechanism. Include major tests over the next 1–3 months when they bear on the medium-term view.
State UTC/Beijing dates and times where verified, scheduling certainty, the sourced expectation or prior state, the hypothesis tested, and the confirming or adverse outcome. Distinguish confirmed schedules, estimated block/epoch timings, conditional milestones, and unscheduled risks. Do not invent exact dates for uncertain developments.
If the following month has already begun, identify completed events as observations and restrict the forward calendar to its remaining period and the separately stated medium-term horizon.

Define approximately three medium-term hypotheses for subsequent monthly editions, each with the original supporting evidence, an observable confirmation milestone, an invalidation condition, and an explicit horizon. These hypotheses should connect price expectations to demand, economic delivery, supply, or risk pricing rather than simply predict higher token prices.
Explain which evidence would justify raising or lowering a theme's research priority. Preserve the original conditions for later evaluation when this report is available; a one-month review must not prematurely score an explicitly longer-lived hypothesis.

## 10. Assessment Review and Final Conclusions
When an actual previous monthly report is available, assess its original hypotheses, trigger conditions, publication time, and validity horizons. A relevant older thesis may be reviewed within its original horizon; a separate planning report is not required. Use a compact comparison of original hypothesis, trigger, observed post-publication outcome, evaluation, and necessary revision where useful.
Evaluate direction, mechanism, timing, and risk warnings separately. Use supported, partially supported, invalidated, not triggered, and still awaiting validation. Do not shorten a 1–3-month horizon to one month, score pre-publication moves, rewrite an earlier condition after the fact, or mark an untriggered scenario as a win or loss.
If no prior report exists in the available context, omit forecast scoring rather than inventing a track record; still complete the market review, forward scenarios, and final conclusions.

Conclude with:
One assessment of the medium-term environment;
Three areas worth sustained research;
Three variables determining the next phase;
The strongest counterevidence;
One question that cannot yet be answered reliably.

[IV. Writing Requirements]

Write in Chinese, retaining necessary English tickers and identifiers. Target approximately 3,000–4,500 Chinese characters for the main text.
Prioritize changes in the medium-term environment, economic evidence, valuation expectations, and vulnerabilities. Integrate a substantive next-month and 1–3-month plan within the same report, without duplicating the retrospective analysis. Broader coverage should not become a catalogue of tokens or a compilation of weekly news.
End with one sentence answering: “Did this month change the demand outlook, token-holder economics, the risk premium the market accepts, or a combination—and which evidence matters most next?”

Lead with conclusions. Prioritize changes, evidence, disagreements, and validation rather than a long news digest or indicator inventory.
If web access is unavailable, say so and do not simulate current research from training memory. Complete only the verifiable portions and distinguish any non-current framework from live findings.
Do not ask me to supply a token universe, market data files, or earlier reports. Use an available user watchlist as additional context, not as a replacement for market-wide coverage; otherwise select a disclosed liquid-market reference universe.
Review earlier hypotheses only when the actual prior report is available in the conversation or accessible authorized context. Never invent earlier views or a forecasting record. Each edition includes both review and outlook; it does not require a separate companion report. Do not imply that alerts, monitoring, trades, or future reports have been scheduled by writing this report.

[Markdown and Formatting Rules]

1. Use standard Markdown for the report body. Use one # title and ## main sections, translating section names naturally into Chinese. Prefer ordinary paragraphs; avoid unnecessary deeper headings, decorative separators, bold, italics, code blocks, HTML, and nested lists.

2. Use tables where explicitly requested or where they materially improve comparison. Keep cells short, with one record per row and no embedded line breaks or lists. Leave a blank line before and after a table. Do not put the vertical-bar character inside cell text. Put dense sourcing in the corresponding paragraph after the table rather than breaking the table layout.

3. Cite sources close to key figures, news facts, and evidence-based assessments. Use the platform's supported citations; when these are unavailable, provide readable source names and working source links. Never print raw internal reference IDs, tool fields, unfinished citation syntax, search placeholders, or empty “Sources” sections.

4. Each paragraph should usually contain 2–5 sentences and develop one idea. Use short lists only for scenarios, event calendars, and final watch items. Explain a fact fully once; later sections should develop its implication rather than repeat the same news.

5. The section order is a research framework, not a requirement to pad the report. Shorten or omit explicitly conditional sections when there is no material evidence. Do not omit core conclusions, period definitions, main supporting and opposing evidence, or the final validation conditions. Prioritize substantive completeness over mechanical word counts while keeping the simplest readable format.
